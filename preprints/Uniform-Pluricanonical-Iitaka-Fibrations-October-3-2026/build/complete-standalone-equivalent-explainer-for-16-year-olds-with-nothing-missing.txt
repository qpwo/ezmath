The question is simple to state: can the dimension of an algebraic shape,
by itself, tell us how far to look in its canonical equations before we
have found all the information those equations can ever reveal?

The manuscript claims yes. It also claims a uniform bound for certain
canonical torsion orders. The two claims are proved together.

This guide explains the definitions, every main intermediate statement,
the quantitative calculations, and how the arguments fit together. It
does not assume that you have the TeX beside you. However, "standalone"
needs an honest qualification: the manuscript imports deep theorems,
including results from companion manuscripts. Their needed statements
are explained here, but their proofs are not supplied in the source and
are not manufactured here. This is an explanation of the supplied
argument, not an independent certification of those external results or
of the manuscript's claimed resolution of the conjecture.

In particular, LA below is a major all-dimensional log-abundance input,
including nonvanishing. It must not be confused with the established
finite-generation theorem for big adjoints. The conclusion of this guide

1. THE QUESTION, WITH THE ESSENTIAL VOCABULARY
======================================================================

1.1 Algebraic shapes and rational information

A variety is a geometric object described by polynomial equations.
"Integral" means it has one irreducible piece and no nilpotent thickening.
"Projective" means it sits as a closed algebraic subset of projective
space; over the complex numbers this supplies an important compactness
property. "Smooth" means its local geometry has no singular points.

Dimension counts independent local coordinates. Complex dimension n
corresponds to real dimension 2n at smooth points.

The field k(X) of rational functions on X consists of fractions of
polynomial-type functions, identified when they agree on a dense open
set. A rational map may be undefined on a smaller closed subset.
Two varieties are birational when dense open parts are isomorphic,
equivalently when their function fields are isomorphic over k.

A birational map is therefore not necessarily an everywhere-defined
isomorphism. Most of this paper studies information unchanged by such
maps.

1.2 Canonical forms and their powers

In n local coordinates, a top differential form looks like

                    f(x) dx_1  ...  dx_n.

The wedge symbol  denotes an alternating product of differentials.
Coordinate changes transform these forms by a determinant. The canonical
line bundle packages that transformation law globally. Its divisor
class is written K_X.

An m-canonical or pluricanonical form is a section of the mth tensor
power of this line bundle. The tensor power is not a differential form
of degree mn on X; it is a power of the line of top forms.

Write

                    H^0(X,mK_X)

for the vector space of global m-canonical sections. A basis s_0,...,s_N
defines the rational map

                    x - [s_0(x): ... :s_N(x)].

Changing the local frame multiplies all entries equally, so the
projective coordinates make sense. The complete linear system |mK_X|
means that we use the whole section space, not a selected subsystem.

Ratios s_i/s_j are rational functions. They record exactly the function
field of this map's image.

1.3 Kodaira dimension and the Iitaka field

If every positive pluricanonical section space is zero, the Kodaira
dimension k(X) is -. Otherwise k(X) is the maximum dimension of a
pluricanonical image. It lies between 0 and dim X.

The Iitaka field is

 K(K_X) = the subfield of k(X) generated by all ratios s/t,
          where s,t are nonzero sections in the same positive degree,
          allowing every such degree.

In sufficiently large divisible degrees the pluricanonical map captures
this entire field. Its connected-fibre birational version is called the
Iitaka fibration.

The important word is "entire." Matching the image dimension is weaker.
For example, C(x^2) is a proper subfield of C(x), even though both have
transcendence degree one. The map x - x^2 has the right image dimension
but loses a twofold choice. This paper insists that no such finite
ambiguity remains.

1.4 The first claimed theorem

For every d >= 1 there is a positive integer m(d) such that every smooth
integral projective d-dimensional variety with k(X) >= 0, over any
algebraically closed characteristic-zero field, satisfies:

 * H^0(X,m(d)K_X) is nonzero;
 * its section ratios generate exactly K(K_X).

The integer depends only on dimension, not on X or on k(X).

At k=0 the required image is a point, so the substantive conclusion is
nonemptiness in the common degree. At k=d, X is "of general type," and
the map is birational. Intermediate values describe genuine fibrations.

This is an existence claim, not a practical numerical recipe for m(d).

2. DIVISORS, SINGULARITIES, AND THE PRECISE TWO CLAIMS
======================================================================

2.1 Divisors and three different meanings of "zero"

A prime divisor is an irreducible codimension-one subvariety. A Weil
divisor is a finite integer combination of prime divisors. A rational
divisor allows rational coefficients. It is effective if all
coefficients are nonnegative.

A rational function h has a principal divisor div(h): its zeros minus
its poles, with their orders.

A Cartier divisor is locally given by one nonzero rational equation.
It corresponds to a line bundle. A rational Cartier divisor means a
rational divisor with some positive integral multiple Cartier.

For rational divisors:

 D ~ 0       means D is an integral principal divisor in the index
             conclusions under discussion.
 D ~_Q 0     means some positive integral multiple is principal.
 D  0       means numerical triviality: relevant intersection
             numbers vanish.

The last two statements do not provide a uniform principal multiple.
Nor does knowing that pD is Cartier tell us that pD is principal.
The paper must control both denominators and torsion.

The index of K_X, when K_X ~_Q 0, is the least r>0 with rK_X principal.
A bound r<=R gives a common killing multiple by taking lcm(1,...,R);
it does not mean that R itself kills every index.

For an integral Weil divisor D on a normal variety,

 H^0(X,D) = {h  k(X): div(h)+D >= 0}  {0}.

This is the divisorial, rank-one reflexive interpretation. It still
makes sense when D is not Cartier. Normality allows regularity to be
checked at generic points of prime divisors.

The notation D  E means E-D is rationally linearly equivalent to an
effective rational divisor. Such a comparison can be restricted only
to a subvariety not contained in that effective divisor's support.

2.2 Positivity

An ample divisor supplies an embedding after taking a sufficiently
large multiple. A nef divisor has nonnegative degree on every curve.
A semiample divisor has a multiple whose sections generate its line
bundle everywhere, giving a morphism.
