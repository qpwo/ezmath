THE QUESTION AND THE WARNING

Can the dimension of a shape defined by polynomial equations tell us
how far to look in its canonical equations before we have found all
the information those equations can ever reveal?

The manuscript claims yes. It simultaneously claims a uniform bound
on certain canonical torsion orders. This guide explains its statements,
calculations, and dependencies; it does not independently certify them.

Four companion preprints matter: Log abundance in characteristic zero
(LA), Arithmetic Stein-degree bounds for log Calabi-Yau pairs (SD),
Uniform effective log Iitaka fibrations for fourfolds (U4), and Relative
denominators and effective systems for log Calabi-Yau fibrations (R4).
In particular, LA supplies all-dimensional log abundance AND
nonvanishing. Neither is inferred here from finite generation for big
adjoints. The conclusions below are conditional on the validity and
applicability of the stated inputs.

"Standalone" means that definitions, the proof's architecture, and its
quantitative comparisons are explained here. It does not mean that the
many imported research theorems are proved from school mathematics.
Read sections 1-3 for the question and strategy; later sections explain
the machinery. The final section identifies every cited source.

1. SHAPES, FORMS, FIELDS: WHAT IS BEING CLAIMED?
==============================================================

A variety is a shape described by polynomial equations. "Integral"
means one irreducible piece, with no nilpotent thickening. "Projective"
means it is a closed subvariety of projective space, whose coordinates
are ratios [x_0:...:x_N]; over the complex numbers it is compact.
"Smooth" means no singular points. Dimension counts independent local
coordinates; complex dimension n means real dimension 2n at smooth
points.

A field permits addition, multiplication, and division by nonzero
elements. The function field k(X) consists of rational functions on X.
